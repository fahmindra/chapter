## What are binomials

A binomial refers to a [polynomial](https://algebrica.org/polynomials) that contains exactly two non-zero terms. Its general form is expressed as $\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)$ or $\left(\right. a - b \left.\right)$.

In this context, $a$ and $b$ represent non-zero, unlike terms, meaning they cannot be combined into a single term. The degree of a binomial corresponds to the highest degree among its terms. For example, $x^{3} + 2$ is a binomial of degree 3, whereas $3 x - 5$ is a binomial of degree $1$.

Binomials exhibit properties that facilitate algebraic manipulation. Among these properties are [notable products](https://algebrica.org/notable-products), which are specific products involving [powers](https://algebrica.org/powers), binomials, and [trinomials](https://algebrica.org/trinomials). These products are fundamental for solving equations and for identifying common mathematical patterns.

## Multiplying two binomials: The FOIL Method

When multiplying two binomials, such as $\left(\right. a + b \left.\right) \left(\right. c + d \left.\right)$ we use the FOIL method to expand the expression. FOIL is an acronym that helps remember the four steps:

- F (First): multiply the first terms: $a \cdot c$
- O (Outer): multiply the outer terms: $a \cdot d$
- I (Inner): multiply the inner terms: $b \cdot c$
- L (Last): multiply the last terms: $b \cdot d$

Putting it all together we have:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right) \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) = a c + a d + b c + b d$$

The FOIL method applies exclusively to the multiplication of two binomials. For products involving polynomials with more than two terms, the general distributive property should be used.

> This method provides an efficient and systematic approach to expanding binomials, particularly when each binomial contains two terms. It ensures comprehensive multiplication and maintains organisational clarity throughout the process.

## Example 1

To see a practical example of multiplying two binomials, let’s consider the expression:
$$\left(\right. x + 3 \left.\right) \left(\right. x + 5 \left.\right)$$

We can apply the FOIL method, which helps recall the correct order of steps when multiplying the terms of each binomial.

- First, multiply the first terms: $x \cdot x = x^{2}$.
- Then, multiply the outer terms: $x \cdot 5 = 5 x$.
- Next, multiply the inner terms: $3 \cdot x = 3 x$.
- Finally, multiply the last terms: $3 \cdot 5 = 15$.

Combining all the results, we get:
 $$x^{2} + 5 x + 3 x + 15 = x^{2} + 8 x + 15$$

The final result shows that:
 $$\left(\right. x + 3 \left.\right) \left(\right. x + 5 \left.\right) = x^{2} + 8 x + 15$$

## Example 2

Let’s now look at another example that involves the product of two binomials containing [complex numbers](https://algebrica.org/complex-numbers-introduction/). Consider the following expression:

$$\left(\right. 2 x - i \left.\right) \left(\right. x + 4 i \left.\right)$$

We can apply the FOIL method, following the same order of multiplication.

- First, multiply the first terms: $2 x \cdot x = 2 x^{2}$.
- Then, multiply the outer terms: $2 x \cdot 4 i = 8 x i$.
- Next, multiply the inner terms: $- i \cdot x = - x i$.
- Finally, multiply the last terms: $- i \cdot 4 i = - 4 i^{2}$.

Combining all results, we get:
 $$2 x^{2} + 8 x i - x i - 4 i^{2}$$

Now simplify by combining like terms and recalling that $i^{2} = - 1$:
 $$2 x^{2} + 7 x i - 4 \left(\right. - 1 \left.\right) = 2 x^{2} + 7 x i + 4$$

The final result shows that:
 $$\left(\right. 2 x - i \left.\right) \left(\right. x + 4 i \left.\right) = 2 x^{2} + 7 x i + 4$$

## Associative, distributive and commutative properties

The associative property states that when adding or multiplying three or more binomials, the grouping does not affect the final result. For addition, given three binomials $\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)$, $\left(\right. c + d \left.\right)$, and $\left(\right. e + f \left.\right)$, the following holds:

$$\left(\right. \left(\right. a + b \left.\right) + \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) \left.\right) + \left(\right. e + f \left.\right) = \left(\right. a + b \left.\right) + \left(\right. \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) + \left(\right. e + f \left.\right) \left.\right)$$

For multiplication:

$$\left(\right. \left(\right. a + b \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. e + f \left.\right) = \left(\right. a + b \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. e + f \left.\right) \left.\right)$$

In both cases, the grouping of the binomials can be changed freely without
altering the overall result.

---

The distributive property is a principle that establishes the connection between multiplication and addition or subtraction. Specifically, it states that multiplying a term by the sum or difference of two other terms is equivalent to distributing the multiplication over each term. Formally:

$$a \left(\right. b + c \left.\right) & = a b + a c \\ a \left(\right. b - c \left.\right) & = a b - a c$$

In these expressions, $a$, $b$, and $c$ may represent real numbers, variables, or more complex algebraic expressions. For example, when applied to a binomial:

$$x \left(\right. x + 3 \left.\right) = x^{2} + 3 x$$

The distributive property serves as a foundational tool for expanding and [factoring](https://algebrica.org/factoring-ac-method/) expressions, solving equations, and simplifying algebraic expressions.

---

The commutative property states that the order of terms in the addition or multiplication of binomials does not influence the final outcome. For example, given two binomials $\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)$ and $\left(\right. c + d \left.\right)$, exchanging their order does not change the overall value.

In the case of addition:
$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right) + \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) = \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) + \left(\right. a + b \left.\right)$$

In the case of multiplication:
$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) = \left(\right. c + d \left.\right) \cdot \left(\right. a + b \left.\right)$$

In both operations, interchanging the order of the two binomials does not alter the result.

> These structural principles extend beyond binomials and originate from the foundational algebraic structure of the real number system. A detailed formal development is available in the section on [properties of real numbers](https://algebrica.org/properties-of-real-numbers/).

## Special cases: notable products

Two fundamental examples of [notable products](https://algebrica.org/notable-products) resulting from binomial multiplication include the following:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{2} = a^{2} + 2 a b + b^{2}$$
$$\left(\right. a - b \left.\right) \left(\right. a + b \left.\right) = a^{2} - b^{2}$$

These identities result from repeated application of the distributive property and illustrate recurring algebraic patterns. They are essential for algebraic expansion, simplification, and factorisation. A comprehensive and systematic discussion is provided on the related page.

## Expansion of a binomial expression

For any [natural number](https://algebrica.org/natural-numbers) $n$, the expansion of a binomial $\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{n}$ is given by the [binomial theorem](https://algebrica.org/binomial-theorem):

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{n} = \sum_{k = 0}^{n} \left(\right. \frac{n}{k} \left.\right) a^{n - k} b^{k}$$

Where $\left(\right. \frac{n}{k} \left.\right)$ represents the [binomial coefficient](https://algebrica.org/binomial-coefficient/) calculated as:
 $$\left(\right. \frac{n}{k} \left.\right) = \frac{n !}{k ! \left(\right. n - k \left.\right) !}$$

The sum indicates that all terms are summed for $k$ ranging from $0$ to $n$ and $a^{n - k} b^{k}$ represents the partial terms of the expansion.

---

To better understand how the expansion of a binomial works through the binomial theorem, let’s expand the following expression using the formula:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{5}$$

According to the binomial theorem, we can write:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{5} = \sum_{k = 0}^{5} \left(\right. \frac{5}{k} \left.\right) a^{5 - k} b^{k}$$

Expanding each term step by step:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{5} & = \left(\right. \frac{5}{0} \left.\right) a^{5} b^{0} + \left(\right. \frac{5}{1} \left.\right) a^{4} b^{1} + \left(\right. \frac{5}{2} \left.\right) a^{3} b^{2} + \left(\right. \frac{5}{3} \left.\right) a^{2} b^{3} + \left(\right. \frac{5}{4} \left.\right) a^{1} b^{4} + \left(\right. \frac{5}{5} \left.\right) a^{0} b^{5} \\ & = 1 \cdot a^{5} + 5 a^{4} b + 10 a^{3} b^{2} + 10 a^{2} b^{3} + 5 a b^{4} + 1 \cdot b^{5}$$

Combining all the terms, we get the expanded form:

$$\left(\right. a + b \left.\right)^{5} = a^{5} + 5 a^{4} b + 10 a^{3} b^{2} + 10 a^{2} b^{3} + 5 a b^{4} + b^{5}$$

This example shows how the binomial theorem provides a systematic way to expand powers of a binomial expression, where each coefficient corresponds to a term in the fifth row of [Pascal’s triangle](https://algebrica.org/binomial-coefficient/).
